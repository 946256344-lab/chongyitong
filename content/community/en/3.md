## Background

Biscuit is a 12-year-old orange tabby who had been eating less and losing weight over the past two months — nearly 400 grams in total. His owner, Sarah, brought him in for a full blood panel. When she got the results, she couldn't understand a word of it. The vet explained a lot, but the only thing that stuck was one word: **kidney failure**.

What she submitted to Pet Med-Pal included the blood test image, a description of Biscuit's recent condition, and her core question:

> "The vet mentioned dialysis and conservative treatment, but didn't say which one is right for Biscuit. I don't know how to choose — or how much time we have left together."

---

## Understanding the Report

Our specialist reviewed the bloodwork and translated the key values into plain language:

| Marker | Biscuit's Value | Reference Range | What It Means |
|--------|----------------|-----------------|---------------|
| BUN (Blood Urea Nitrogen) | 58 mg/dL | 14–36 | Elevated — kidneys aren't clearing waste efficiently |
| Creatinine | 3.8 mg/dL | 0.8–2.4 | Significantly high — indicates notable kidney damage |
| Phosphorus | 6.1 mg/dL | 2.6–5.0 | High — dietary phosphorus restriction needed |
| Potassium | 3.2 mEq/L | 3.5–5.0 | Low — supplementation recommended |

Based on the IRIS (International Renal Interest Society) staging system, Biscuit is at **Stage 3 CKD**. Kidney function is significantly impaired, but this is not yet end-stage — there is still meaningful room to slow progression.

---

## Treatment Options

**Option 1: Active Management (Subcutaneous fluids + dietary changes + regular monitoring)**
- Best for: owners who can commit to 1–2 subcutaneous fluid sessions per week, or learn to do it at home
- Expected outcome: stabilize condition and slow progression; some cats maintain good quality of life for 2–3 years
- Estimated cost: $100–200/month (includes recheck bloodwork and fluid supplies)

**Option 2: Dialysis**
- Best for: short-term support during acute crises — not a long-term solution for chronic disease
- Limitations: highly stressful for senior cats; feline dialysis is not widely available
- Recommendation: unless there is an acute episode, Option 1 is preferred

**Option 3: Palliative / Comfort Care**
- Best for: owners with limited capacity, or cats already showing significant distress
- Focus: quality nutrition, stress reduction, and comfort — prioritizing the time remaining

---

## Questions to Bring to Your Vet

Our specialist compiled this checklist for Sarah to go through at her next appointment:

1. What is Biscuit's current IRIS stage, and is there proteinuria present?
2. Do you recommend starting home subcutaneous fluid therapy? What's the dose and frequency?
3. Should we switch to a prescription renal diet? Which brands do you recommend?
4. Phosphorus is elevated — do we need a phosphorus binder?
5. Potassium is low — what's the best way to supplement: oral or injection?
6. How often should we recheck bloodwork, and which trends are most important to watch?

---

## Outcome

Sarah brought the checklist to her next appointment and had a 40-minute conversation with her vet — something she described as far more productive than any visit before. The vet set up a home fluid protocol and recommended a low-phosphorus prescription diet.

Three months later, Sarah reported that Biscuit's appetite had improved, he had regained about 200 grams, and his creatinine had dropped from 3.8 to 3.1.

> "Before, I just nodded and hoped for the best. Now I actually understand what the numbers mean — and I'm not afraid to ask questions anymore."
